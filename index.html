How binaural beats work

Each ear gets a slightly different pure tone. With 245 Hz in the left ear and 255 Hz in the right, your brainstem combines them and you hear a soft pulsing at the difference: 10 Hz. That pulse does not exist in the air. It is created inside your auditory system, which is why it only works with headphones.

Each preset copies the exact settings of the strongest controlled study we found for its band: the same tones in each ear, the same background, the same balance between tones and noise, and the same listening time. The study details are listed below.

Two of those studies played the tones louder than the noise, which can feel sharp. The Monroe Institute's Hemi-Sync takes the opposite approach and layers the tones under pink noise, music or ocean surf. If a preset feels harsh, lower Beat volume until the tones sit under the noise. That moves away from the study settings but is often more comfortable.

The idea behind using them is that brain activity may follow the pulse, nudging it toward the rhythm linked to a state like sleep or focus. Brain rhythms are grouped into bands by frequency, and each preset targets one band.

The honest summary of the research: some controlled studies find small benefits for anxiety, attention and memory, but results are inconsistent, studies are mostly small, and evidence that the brain actually locks onto the beat is mixed. Treat this as a pleasant, low-risk tool to try, not a treatment.

Noise colors

Optional masking layer under the beat

White noise

Equal energy at every frequency, so it sounds bright and hissy, like TV static. It is the harshest color, so it plays a little quieter and with the top hiss rounded off. Some studies report better attention in people with ADHD while listening to white noise.

Pink noise

Energy falls off as pitch rises, so it sounds balanced, like steady rain. This is the background most often used in binaural beat studies, and it has also been studied for deep sleep.

Surf

Soft pink noise that swells and recedes every 10 seconds or so, like waves on a beach. Ocean surf is one of the beds the Monroe Institute uses under its Hemi-Sync beats. The only bed that changes over time; pick white, pink or brown for a perfectly steady sound.

Brown noise

Even more weight in the low end, a deep rumble like a waterfall or airplane cabin. Popular for sleep and focus, with little formal research behind it yet. Many people find it the least tiring.

Before you listen

  • Headphones are required. Speakers will not produce the beat.
  • Keep the volume low. Long listening at high volume can damage hearing.
  • Do not use while driving or doing anything that needs full alertness, especially the sleep and meditation settings.
  • If you have epilepsy, a seizure disorder or a heart condition, or you use a pacemaker, talk to a doctor first.
  • This is not a medical device and does not treat any condition.
  • On phones, sound may stop when the screen locks. Keep this page open in the foreground.
